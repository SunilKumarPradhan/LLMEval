## 🧭 Overview

This session (Part 2) builds on the earlier discussion of LLM benchmarks and leaderboards. While Part 1 explained *what* benchmarks and leaderboards are, this part answers the practical question:

> **"How do I actually USE a leaderboard to pick the right LLM for my application?"**

**Prerequisites:** Basic understanding of LLM benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, etc.), what leaderboards are (LM Arena, MTEB, Artificial Analysis, etc.), and the concept of confidence intervals in evaluation scores.

**Why this matters:** Most developers make the mistake of blindly picking the "#1 ranked model" on a leaderboard without checking whether that model actually fits their use case, budget, deployment constraints, or task type. This leads to poor real-world performance, wasted budget, or non-compliant deployments (e.g., picking a closed-source API model when your organization needs on-premise/self-hosted models).

This session gives you a **systematic, bias-free framework** to select LLMs using leaderboards the *right* way.

---

## ⚠️ The Problem: Bias Toward Rank #1

> **Definition:** Rank-1 bias is the tendency of developers to assume the top-ranked model on any leaderboard is automatically the best choice for their specific application — without checking if it fits their real constraints.

**Why this is dangerous:**
- The #1 model might be a **closed-source, cloud-only API** (e.g., a proprietary model), but your company policy might require **on-premise (self-hosted)** deployment.
- The #1 model might be **extremely expensive** per token, which fails for a budget-constrained startup.
- The #1 model might excel at **general chat** but be terrible at **RAG embedding tasks** or **agentic tool use** — the areas your product actually needs.

**Real-world example from transcript:** If your requirement is strictly on-premise deployment, you **cannot** use a model like "Claude" (a closed API-only model) — no matter how high it ranks — because it's not deployable on-premise. This constraint should automatically push you toward **open-source models** instead.

**The fix:** Before touching any leaderboard, write down your own hard constraints clearly. This removes emotional/hype-driven bias and makes your model selection **objective**.

## 🪜 The 5-Step Framework to Use LLM Leaderboards

This is the core, practical takeaway of the session — a repeatable process any team can follow.

### 🔹 Step 1: Know Your Own Requirements First

Before opening any leaderboard, clearly write down:
- **Deployment mode:** Cloud API vs On-Premise (self-hosted)
- **Budget constraints:** Cost per token / inference cost limits
- **Task type:** Chatbot, RAG, Agent, Coding, Summarization, etc.
- **Latency requirements:** Real-time vs batch processing
- **Data privacy/compliance needs:** Can data leave your infra or not?

> **Why this step exists:** Once these constraints are crystal clear, your brain automatically filters out irrelevant options. E.g., "I need on-premise" → "I cannot use closed API models like Claude" → "I must look at open-source models only." This mental clarity prevents rank-1 bias and makes you think **objectively** instead of chasing hype.

---

### 🔹 Step 2: Go to the RIGHT Leaderboard (Not Any Random One)

Different leaderboards measure different things. Match the leaderboard to your **actual task**.

| Your Use Case | Leaderboard to Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Building an **Agent** | Agent-specific leaderboards | Measures tool-use, planning, multi-step task success |
| Building a **Chatbot** | LM Arena (Chatbot Arena) | Gives direct, objective human-preference measurement of chat quality |
| Building **RAG** | MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) | RAG needs embedding models — MTEB ranks embedding models specifically |
| **Budget-constrained** application | Artificial Analysis / similar cost-focused leaderboards | Shows exact speed (tokens/sec) and cost ($ per million tokens) tradeoffs |

> **Key Insight:** A general-purpose leaderboard (like overall MMLU rankings) is **not always relevant**. Task-specific leaderboards give you a much more meaningful signal for your specific application.

---

### 🔹 Step 3: Read the Leaderboard Correctly (Don't Just Trust the Number)

This is the most detail-heavy step. A single rank/score number on a leaderboard hides a LOT of context. Before trusting it, dig into:

1. **What exactly is being scored?** (accuracy? human preference? win-rate?)
2. **How is it being scored?** (methodology — MCQ accuracy, pairwise comparison, LLM-as-judge, etc.)
3. **Who evaluated it?** (the model creator themselves = potential bias, vs independent third party)
4. **What was the inference budget?** (was reasoning/chain-of-thought allowed? how many tokens were used?)
5. **Is reasoning mode on or off?** (models perform very differently with/without reasoning enabled)
6. **How old is the evaluation dataset?** Is it being updated regularly?
7. **Is there a private, unseen test set maintained?** (prevents overfitting/data contamination)
8. **Are the benchmarks saturated?** (i.e., most models scoring 95%+ means the benchmark can no longer differentiate models meaningfully)
9. **Are confidence intervals reported?**
   - If a small dataset was used, the confidence interval could be wide.
   - If two models' scores are very close AND confidence intervals overlap, they are **effectively the same** — don't pick one over the other just because of a 0.5% difference.
10. **If it's a composite leaderboard** (combining multiple benchmarks into one score) — **what weightage is given to each capability?** A model might score high overall but weak in exactly the capability you need.

> **Practical tip from transcript:** Read the **footnotes/star marks (*)**, the **"Key Definitions"** sections, and the **FAQ** sections of leaderboard websites. These sections explain methodology and help you judge the leaderboard's credibility and relevance — most people skip these and just look at the ranking number, which is a mistake.

---

### 🔹 Step 4: Shortlist Top 3–5 Models

Based on everything analyzed in Steps 1–3 (your requirements + right leaderboard + correct reading of methodology), narrow down your options to a **shortlist of 3 to 5 candidate models**.

> This is NOT your final answer yet — it's a **filtered candidate pool**.

---

### 🔹 Step 5: Run Your OWN Custom Evaluation (Most Important Step)

> **This is the single most important step in the entire framework.**

Take your shortlisted 3–5 models and run **your own custom evaluation** using:
- Your own evaluation dataset (representative of your real use case)
- Your own success metrics
- Your own real-world scenarios/prompts

Whichever model performs best on **your custom eval** is your **true top candidate** for your application — not necessarily the one ranked #1 on the public leaderboard.

> **Why:** Public leaderboards test general capabilities on generic datasets. They can never perfectly simulate YOUR specific data distribution, domain vocabulary, edge cases, and success criteria. Only your own evaluation can do that.

## 🏆 The Golden Rule: Filtering Tool, Not Decision Tool

> **"Leaderboards are not a selection tool. Leaderboards are a filtering tool, not a decision tool."**

This is explicitly called out as **the most important line to remember from this entire session**.

**What it means:**
- ❌ **Wrong approach:** Open a leaderboard → see rank #1 → use that model directly in production.
- ✅ **Right approach:** Use leaderboards to **narrow down** hundreds of models to a **shortlist of 4-5**, then run **your own custom evaluations** on that shortlist to make the **final decision**.

```
[All LLMs in the world]
        │
        ▼  (Step 1: Apply your constraints - budget, deployment, task type)
[Filtered by requirements]
        │
        ▼  (Step 2 & 3: Use correct leaderboard + read it properly)
[Ranked candidates on relevant leaderboard]
        │
        ▼  (Step 4: Shortlist)
[Top 3-5 candidate models]
        │
        ▼  (Step 5: Run YOUR custom evaluation) ★ FINAL DECISION MAKER ★
[The ONE model for your application]
```
