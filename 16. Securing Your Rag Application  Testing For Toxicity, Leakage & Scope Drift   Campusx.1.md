## 🎯 Overview

This lecture is part of an ongoing series on building a **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) Evaluation Suite**. Having already covered **component evals**, **pipeline evals (RAG Triad)**, and **quality evals** (correctness, completeness, style), this session begins the **Safety Evals** module — arguably the most important and most nuanced part of evaluating any LLM-based application.

> **Why this matters:** LLM applications are being deployed at scale in the real world. Unlike traditional deterministic software, LLMs are **probabilistic** — meaning the same input can produce different outputs every time. This unpredictability makes safety a much harder problem than in classic software engineering, and it directly impacts user trust, brand reputation, legal liability, and even financial loss.

## 🛡️ What is LLM Safety? <a name="what-is-llm-safety"></a>

> **Definition:** LLM Safety means that after building an application using an LLM, you (the developer) and your users can use that application **safely** — nothing unexpected happens, and nothing degrades the user experience.

In simple terms: You built a chatbot/agent — you want to make sure it doesn't do something harmful, embarrassing, illegal, or unintended, either to your business or to your users.

Safety is **not a new concept** in software — it existed for Android apps, websites, and desktop applications long before LLMs. That earlier discipline gave birth to **Cyber Security** as a full field.

> **Prediction from the instructor:** Similarly, within the next ~5 years, **LLM Safety & Security** will emerge as its own independent sub-field within AI/CS — complete with dedicated job roles, researchers, and specialists whose entire career revolves around securing LLM-based systems. Terms like **Guardrails** and **Governance** are already part of this emerging field.

## ⚙️ Why LLM Safety is Harder Than Traditional Software Security <a name="why-harder"></a>

Traditional software is **deterministic** — same input, same output, always. This makes it relatively easier to test, secure, and patch.

LLMs, by nature, are **probabilistic**:

> Same input → Different output, every single time.

This single characteristic is why securing LLM-based software **at scale** is significantly trickier than securing purely deterministic software systems. You can't just write a fixed set of "if-else" security rules — you need statistical, evaluation-driven, and layered defense approaches.

## 🚨 LLM Safety Failure Modes <a name="failure-modes"></a>

These are the most **common ways** an LLM-based application can fail. This is not an exhaustive list of the entire attack surface — but it covers what you'll encounter most frequently in the real world.

### 1️⃣ Sensitive Information Leakage

> LLMs are trained on huge amounts of data, and their parametric knowledge (encoded in weights & biases) contains a vast, often unknown, mix of information.

Attackers use clever techniques to extract:
- System prompts
- Private/personal data
- Credentials (API keys, passwords)
- Proprietary content

**Why it happens:** Nobody fully knows what knowledge is "hidden" inside a trained model's weights — smart prompting can coax it out.

### 2️⃣ Scope Drift / Policy Violation

The application is forced to operate **outside its intended purpose** or violate its own policies.

**Real-world example:** Amazon launched a chatbot (~3 years ago) meant purely for **sales support**. Some students, upon losing access to their paid ChatGPT subscription, discovered they could manipulate Amazon's chatbot using clever prompts to do their **homework** instead of answering shopping queries — a clear scope violation.

### 3️⃣ Harmful / Toxic Output

The model generates content that is dangerous, abusive, or toxic.

**Examples from early LLM era:**
- A user got step-by-step instructions to build a bomb using household items.
- Models generated toxic responses full of slurs/abusive language.

> This has reduced significantly with newer models but is not fully eliminated.

### 4️⃣ Misinformation / Hallucination

> The model confidently invents facts, procedures, or events that are completely false.

This is a **core, long-standing problem**. Earlier models used to hallucinate large, elaborate false facts very confidently — making it hard for users to detect the lie.

### 5️⃣ Bias & Unfairness

The same question asked by people from different backgrounds gets **different treatment/quality of response**.

**Root cause:** LLMs are trained on real-world data, and real-world data inherently contains bias — against gender, race, etc. This bias can leak into chatbot responses.

> This is decreasing over time with better training practices, but is **not yet zero**.

### 6️⃣ Unsafe Actions / Excessive Agency

Especially relevant for **agentic systems** with tool access.

> If an agent has access to many tools, it may end up using them in **unauthorized or harmful ways**.

**Real-world example:** News reports of an AI trading agent that broke its guardrails and autonomously invested (and lost) real money in the stock market.
