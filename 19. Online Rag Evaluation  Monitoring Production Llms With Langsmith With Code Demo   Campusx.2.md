## 🔔 Setting Up Alerts in LangSmith {#alerts}

> **Definition**: An **alert** is a rule that monitors a metric continuously and notifies you (e.g., via Slack) when a threshold is breached.

### Example Alert Configured
- **Condition**: Total LLM cost > $5 in the last 60 minutes
- **Action**: Send a message to Slack

### Why Alerts Matter
Without alerts, you'd have to manually check dashboards all day. Alerts let the system **push** notifications to you the moment something goes wrong — e.g., a sudden cost spike (maybe due to a bug causing infinite retries, or a bot spamming your API).

### General Alert Setup Pattern
1. Choose the metric (e.g., total cost, average latency)
2. Set a threshold value
3. Set a time window (e.g., last 60 minutes)
4. Choose a notification channel (Slack, email, etc.)

> **Note**: Average latency alerts *can* be created in LangSmith, but **P95 latency alerts are not currently supported** as a built-in option — this was explicitly called out as a limitation.

## 📊 P95 Latency vs Average Latency {#p95}

This was a key conceptual discussion.

| Metric | What it Represents | Use Case |
|--------|--------------------|----------|
| **Average Latency** | Mean response time across all requests | General health monitoring, good for alerting |
| **P95 Latency** | The latency value below which 95% of requests fall (i.e., the worst 5% take longer than this) | Understanding the experience of your "unhappy"/outlier users |

> **Quote**: *"P95 tells you how sad your sad users are — the ones taking the longest to get an answer."*

**Why P95 exists**: Average latency can hide the pain experienced by a minority of users (e.g., if 95% of requests take 1 second but 5% take 30 seconds, the average might still look fine). P95 surfaces this problem.

**Why P95 isn't used for alerting (in this context)**: 
- P95 represents **outliers**, not the general population
- Alerting on outlier behavior can cause noisy/unnecessary alerts
- LangSmith currently doesn't provide P95-based alerting as a built-in option — only average-based alerting

## 🎯 Service Level Objectives (SLO) {#slo}

> **Definition**: An **SLO (Service Level Objective)** is a business-defined target for how a service should perform — e.g., "95% of responses must return within X seconds."

### Key Insight from Q&A
**Question**: "How do I decide what threshold to set for a 'sensitive' latency alert — should I just use a lower number like 2 minutes instead of 5?"

**Answer**: **No** — the threshold isn't arbitrary. It comes from your **SLO**, which itself comes from your **business use case**:

- A **customer support chatbot** → users expect near-instant replies → SLO might be a few seconds
- A **research assistant** (that researches, then synthesizes) → users tolerate longer wait → SLO might be 1 minute or more

> **Key takeaway**: Alert thresholds are a **business/product decision**, not a technical guess. You don't "think up" a number — you look at what your SLO says and configure the alert to match it.

## 🛡️ Online Safety Evaluation - Toxicity {#safety}

Now moving to the **Safety** layer of online evals — specifically measuring **toxicity** in production responses.

### Two Options to Measure Toxicity Online

| Option | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Option 1** | Reuse the offline toxicity eval you built earlier (e.g., with DeepEval) |
| **Option 2** | Use LangSmith's **built-in online evaluator** for toxicity |

### Why Use LangSmith's Built-in Evaluator for Toxicity (reasoning given)

1. **Demonstration purpose** — to show how online evaluators work in LangSmith
2. **Toxicity is a "detection" metric, not a "comparison" metric** — what matters is *whether* toxicity is detected at all, not the exact scoring methodology. Since LangSmith is built by a large team of researchers, their toxicity detector is likely as good as or better than a custom one.

> Contrast this with **quality metrics** (faithfulness, relevancy) where consistency between offline/online tooling *does* matter — explained in detail later.

## 🧰 Built-in LangSmith Evaluators {#builtin}

LangSmith ships with **reference-free** built-in evaluators (no need for a "correct answer" to compare against) found under **Evaluators** section:

- **PII Leakage** — detects personally identifiable information being leaked
- **Prompt Injection** — detects attempts to hijack the LLM via malicious prompts
- **Toxicity** — detects toxic/harmful content in responses
- **Bias and Fairness**
- **Correctness** (this one IS reference-based, different from the rest)

> Since PII leakage, prompt injection, and toxicity are reference-free, they can be used directly out-of-the-box without needing a golden dataset.

## 🎲 Sampling Rate Concept {#sampling}

> **Definition**: **Sampling rate** determines what percentage of production traces get evaluated by an online evaluator, instead of evaluating every single trace.

### Why Sampling Matters

If your app receives **50,000 queries/day**, running an LLM-as-judge evaluator (like toxicity, powered by GPT models) on *every single trace* means:
- 50,000 extra LLM calls per day, per evaluator
- Significant additional **cost**

### The Statistical Idea

> **Quote**: *"We are trying to estimate the population with a sample."*

Rather than evaluating all 50,000 traces, you can randomly evaluate a **sample** (e.g., 30%) and get a statistically fair estimate of the overall toxicity level in your system — similar to how polling/surveys work.

| Sampling Rate | Meaning |
|---------------|---------|
| 100% | Evaluate every single trace (expensive but complete) |
| 30% | Evaluate ~30 out of every 100 traces (cheaper, still representative) |

> **Production guidance**: The sampling rate you choose depends on your **budget**. In the demo, 100% was used purely to observe results quickly — in real production, a lower rate (like 10-30%) is typical.

## ⚙️ Configuring the Toxicity Evaluator - Step by Step {#configure}

Steps to set up the built-in toxicity evaluator in LangSmith:

1. Go to **Evaluators** tab in your LangSmith project
2. Click **+** and select **Toxicity** under the Safety category
3. **Name** — auto-filled; **Application** — auto-selected
4. **Model (LLM-as-Judge)** — select which model will act as the judge (e.g., GPT-4o-mini). This can be any provider you configure — the demo kept the default.
5. **System Prompt** — editable if you want to customize the toxicity-detection instructions. Recommendation: keep the default since LangSmith's built-in prompt is likely well-tested.
6. **Source selection**: Choose **Tracing** (NOT "Datasets")
   - Selecting "Datasets" → makes it an **offline** evaluation
   - Selecting "Tracing" → makes it an **online** evaluation (runs on live production traces)
7. **Sampling Rate** — set based on budget (100% used in demo for immediate visibility)
8. **API Key** — provide your OpenAI (or relevant provider) key, since the judge LLM calls will cost money
9. Click **Save**

### Result
Once configured, every new trace (or sampled traces) automatically gets evaluated for toxicity. The score appears under **Feedback** on the trace, along with a **written explanation** of why that score was given (e.g., *"The response is explanatory and professional. It contains no personal attacks."* → Score: 0).
